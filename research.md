	<summary><h2><a>Topological Defects in Ordered Phases</a></h2></summary>
		<p>
			Physical systems with underlying order or broken symmetries can exhibit
			textures which cannot be removed by continuous perturbations. These are
			known as topological defects, and can alter the macroscopic properties
			of the physical system of interest!
		</p>
		<h3>Interaction Among Defects and Boundaries in Nematic Liquid Crystals</h3>
		<p>
			How do topological defects in nematic liquid crystals (fluids composed of 
			rodlike molecules) interact with one another and their environment? 
			We use a combination of computation and
			analysis to understand how topological defects in nematics---which are 
			spatially extended lines---behave when interacting or are subject to
			different boundary conditions.
		</p>

		<h3>Topological and Geometric Characterization of Defects in Ordered Phases</h3>
		<p>
			How do we identify topological and geometric features in ordered phases of
			matter? Can we develop theoretical tools for characterizing these textures from
			experimental or computational data? We use tools from algebraic topology and analysis
			to answer these questions in soft matter systems such as nematics, cholesterics, and
			smectics.
		</p>
		<figure>
			<img src="/public/images/research/LoopDefectGeometry.png" width="500" alt="Loop Defects With Different Local Geometry">
			<figcaption>We've developed methods to extract local geometric signatures from defects in uniaxial nematics.</figcaption>
		</figure>

			<img src="/public/images/research/HalfLambdaDefect.png" width="300" alt="A Lambda Defect in a Cholesteric Phase">
			<figcaption>We would like to generalize these methods to defects in more complicated phases like biaxial nematics, cholesterics (shown above), or smectics, for example.</figcaption>
		</figure>
		
		<h3>Nucleation of Topological Defects in Ordered Phases</h3>
		<p>
			Can we predict what topological textures will appear across a phase transition?
			In phase transitions to ordered phases, defect nucleation is governed by the
			Kibble mechanism, where regions of mismatched order grow into each other. While
			the Kibble mechanism tells us why defects appear, it does not tell us what 
			kind of defect will appear. We are interested in applying our mathematical tools 
			and computations to better understand the conditions required for nucleating various 
			types of defects.
		</p>

	<summary><h2><a>Non-equilibrium Phase Transitions in Active Matter</a></h2></summary>
		<p>
			Active nematics have proven to be a fruitful model system for non-equilibrium 
			behavior. Both biological and synthetic experimental active nematic systems 
			have been developed and theoretical models do qualitatively well in comparison
			to experiments. We are interested in testing and developing fundamental methods 
			for understanding non-equilibrium systems using active nematics as a model system.
		</p>
		<h3>Quiescence to Active Turbulence Phase Transition</h3>
		<p>
			What does the active turbulence transition tell us about non-equilibrium phase 
			transtions? We are interested in using the active turbulence transition to test 
			and develop fundamental theories of non-equilibrium physics.
		</p>

		<h3>External Control of Active Nematics</h3>
		<p>
			How can we control active nematics and other non-equilibrium systems? We 
			search for ways to use external boundary conditions, interactions with a
			substrate, or activity patterning to tame the chaos of active turbulence 
			and discover novel non-equilibrium phase transitions that can be analogized

	<summary><h2><a>Modeling Cellular Tissues as Active Matter</a></h2></summary>
		<p>
			The modeling of cellular tissues presents a unique challenge to physicists.
			On one hand, they are incredibly biologically complex with indescribable amounts
			of biochemical processes. On the other hand, at the macroscopic level, they exhibit
			broken symmetries and emergent phenomena, similar to soft and active matter systems
			which have been well-studied. We are interested in using our understanding of 
			soft and active matter to develop as-simple-as-possible models for cellular tissues.
		</p>
		<h3>Cellular Tissues as Active Materials</h3>
		<p>
			How do tissues organize and use their environment to move, expand, and maintain 
			homeostasis? We are interested in developing simple models for cellular tissues
			that recapitulate experimental observations, and can be modified to couple to external
			parameters or competing cell types. One of the key questions is how much biochemical
			detail is necessary to capture the physical processes observed in cellular tissues.
		</p>
		
		<p>
			We've recently worked with an isotropic active matter model to understand how activity
			couples to external asymmetry and shown that anisotropic friction can guide clusters
			of active agents. We were inspired by the behavior of cells in recent experiments on 
			anisotropic substrates, and hope to extend this model in the future.
		</p>
